# Settings

Consider the search for a magick artifact in some faraway mountain ruins, where the characters fight kobolds and barely escape the wrath of a forgotten god of chaos, only to be tricked by an antique dealer in a major port on their way home. Notice how this archetypal plot touches upon no less than ten opposite pairs:

```
   now ↔ before   civilization ↔ wilderness 
  home ↔ away           reason ↔ magick 
person ↔ monster         above ↔ below 
  good ↔ evil          mundane ↔ divine 
  life ↔ death           order ↔ chaos
```

Fantasy campaign settings are often structured around such contrasts, because they make the adventure stand out from everyday life and gives it impetus: action is required because the current state of affairs risks being/needs to be overturned. Concretely, this might be the threat of an returning sorcerer seeking dominion over the world, or the characters starting a revolution to overthrow an evil emperor. But structurally, it grows from the setting, whether implied or explicit, and is the result of tension between opposites. Most adventures move towards the right pole, hoping to succesfully return things to the left pole.[^1] 

To reduce complexity and give players a grip on the setting, many game worlds contain some kind of magick, historic and catalyste catastrophe, which generates opposites and tension in one event. You get a before the catastrophe and after, a fragile civilization struggling in the ruins of the past, wondrous technology that can no longer be replicated, beings and entities warring for dominion, new structures built on top of sunken ones, etc. 

When you design, analyze or spice up a setting, a structural understanding along these lines is often useful as a point of departure for your own ideas.

## Hacking Your Notion of Normal

The expression “normal fantasy” may sound contradictory, but it really isn’t. Like many other popular genres, mainstream fantasy relies on things being familiar. Any player has a number of components—like dwarves having beards and living below ground—that she feels need to be in the world, or she is not getting what she came for. For the game to feel fresh, you should aim to hack *your* group’s notion of what is normal. This is best done with precision and moderation. If nothing is recognizable, nothing sticks out, either. Concentrate on a select few important aspects of the setting and leave the rest! 

Try hacking things like common foes or humanoids, how death works, the nature of gods, magick, the general technological level, the climate or means of traveling. For example, a curse that has left dwarves, elves and halflings looking like marred deviations in the human gene pool and brought a new ice age upon the world (hacking humanoids and climate) can give you a grim mutant campaign about prejudice, persecution and survival. An invasion by insectoids with advanced technology (hacking technology level and common foes) could make a campaign with a colonial theme inspired by history and science fiction. Note how both these suggestions make use of the opposite pairs in the previous section, each introducing them through a catalyste catastrophe.

## Switching or Mixing Genres

The Optional Rules chapter contains rules that can help you play *Whitehack* in other genres than fantasy, such as horror, science fiction or urban fantasy (p. 72). But genres aren’t mere containers for stereotypes, tropes and typical trappings. Turning a crossbow into a ray gun or letting the adventurers travel space instead of the sea doesn’t necessarily make your game science fiction, and the inclusion of a few tentacled monsters won’t turn it into cosmic horror, either.

Genres run deeper: they are formalized answers to historical social needs. If you want to play horror, aim for a setting that challenges boundaries and focuses encounters with the unknown and malevolent. If you want to play science fiction, aim for a future setting that serves as a critique of the present. You could also stay within the realm of fantasy, albeit with a few deviant genre markers as seasoning. Remember that anything you bring into the game will have some kind of genre connotation. You can create very interesting mixes by deliberately picking elements and weighing them against one another. In fact, it is hard to find any well-known popular fiction that doesn’t, to some degree.

## Playing with History

Instead of letting present day or future technology “invade” traditional fantasy, you may consider letting fantasy invade history. What if the plague in Sicily in 1347 has supernatural causes (in reality it arrived from Crimea), and the adventurers try to put an end to it? What if an unknown deep sea empire collapses during the Napoleon Wars, and its scaled, amphibian inhabitants end up in Northern Europe with the knowledge of magick? There is a huge advantage in combining the freedom of fantasy with historic background, especially when you can use as much or as little of that background as you want. Also, history is a very common interest that you are likely to find in your gaming group to some extent.

## Using a Literary Setting

Literary settings from novels that you and the group have read contain a lot of useful material—background, environments, people, creatures, items, maps etc. This is a **huge** advantage—if everyone is familiar with it! Otherwise it can easily create a rift in the gaming group between those who know and those who don’t. 

Great literary worlds and great game worlds aren’t necessarily the same thing, though. An author can come up with an amazing plot that captures the reader, because she gets to make all the choices and only has to show the parts she wants. Consider candidate novels carefully from a game perspective first. Can the setting provide enough variation in its environments, encountered foes and adventure types? Does it provide strong purpose for adventuring? Will the books’ storylines constrict or enrich gameplay?

[^1]: This doesn’t have to imply a reactionary plot. The order you return to can be new, for example, as could the notions of “civilization” or “good.”
