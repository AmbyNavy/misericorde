# Random Tables

Random tables are very prominent in the original rules tradition and essential if you are aiming for an old school play-style. The point of them is to take away some of the Referee’s control over the game world and make it less vulnerable to her whims and biases. It is common to randomize things like loot, which monsters are encountered, weather, which stores are in a town and many other things. If you randomize part of an adventure, you also give it a higher replay value. 

However, creating random tables for everything is an arduous task, especially considering that many entries don’t ever come into play. The random tables are also tied in with the game world, and unless you play in a very generic world or are happy with whatever world gets created through your rolls, you need to either use a pre-generated world or do the work yourself.

- Use short tables and replace used entries. Keep a backlog to modify and recycle into another game. 
- Nestle your tables and combine them in different ways for different purposes. 
- Use imbalanced tables. For example, a loot table may give a great chance for mundane finds, but a tiny chance for exceptional ones. 
- Table entries don’t have to be “complete.” Allow for filling in some details of an entry once it has been rolled. This will go a long way to avoid nonsensical results. 
-  Use the random tables on pp. 108 f. to design ones of your own.

## Modifying Table Results

Seemingly counter to the very point of having random tables, you may sometimes benefit from adjusting the results that you get. The time to do that is **not** when you would rather get a different result (because that would mean choosing instead of rolling), but when the combination of a table roll and the current game world situation unexpectedly results in something that feels artificial, out of place and indeed very random. For example, when playing some old-school module, you get a bird encounter on a dungeon level that you completely flooded, or trigger the summoning of a dragon while crawling through a long 2×2 feet tunnel.

Unless you are specifically after a gonzo or random game, you should as a group discuss what the minimum possible modification of the random result should be, in order to make the game fiction feel right. This does not break the integrity of the dice, but safeguards the point of using them: to get an immersive game.

## Randomizing Input Rather Than Output

By randomizing what goes *into* your creativity as a Referee, you may find it easier to get things *out*—on the fly, during your preparations or when creating other tables. In addition to the tables on the following spread, you also have the group generator (p. 43) and the wording oracle (p. 40). The former is good for coming up with Referee character concepts and factions, while the latter is good for some fine-tuning.

- Use 2–4 of the tables and force yourself to include all the words that you got, in a basic idea. 
- Use another 1–2 tables and again, force your mind to use the new words to give some more detail to the initial idea. 
- Now drop the requirement to include the words and finish what you have started on your own.

Sometimes, the words that you get can be used **metaphorically**. You don’t even need the tables for this. As long as you are prepared to move from a literal to a figurative understanding, you could flip any book and use the first and last word on the page that comes up.

The metaphorical approach is particularly good for dealing with a randomization of **weather**. You can’t make weather completely random anyway, as it depends on the climate, the location, the season and quite simply what the weather was yesterday, so putting an extra filter between the dice and your imagination is a good idea. For example, if you roll for Personality and Drama and you get the words “confused” and “struggle,” perhaps the weather that day is shifty, making it hard for people to know what to wear and what equipment to bring.

Finally, the tables can be used to come up with an adventure concept. Following the above process, use the Items, Origin and Drama tables, as well as the Group Generator (p. 43), for a basic concept of the adventure’s problem or issue. Then use the Locations (any of the two) and Events tables for fleshing out the basic concept.
