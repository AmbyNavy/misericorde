# Campaigns

A campaign is defined by a series of adventures that are connected. Some campaigns are strong stories with a clearly defined start, middle and end, while others are little more than episodes in the lives of a bunch of adventurers. Since *Whitehack* is powered by the collision between collective narration and game world simulation— heroes tend to die more often than not!—the former is what many players dream of while the latter is by far easier to achieve. Then again, can’t a strong story be appreciated by the players, even though no single *character* lives through it from start to finish? And conversely: will a loose set of episodes not connect in the minds of the players, forming a story where their characters progress from having little skill, to heroes taking on tremendous monsters?

## Plots

Good campaign plots take planning. Not all ideas work well for a longer series of sessions. Consider starting with a version of these:

- The Journey. The characters start somewhere and have a destination, which is the peak of the campaign. On the way, they will make stops, choose routes, change traveling means etc. A quest, blazing a trail, exploring/mapping an area are variants of this plot. 
- The Mystery. Each adventure can provide a piece of a grander puzzle. This is a flexible plot that can hold may different adventure types and environments. 
- The Event. Gather a number of components (items, events, people) to stop something, or make it happen. This need not be clear from the start. Consider letting the characters decide what should to be done, at least for part of the campaign. Put some work into the components, or this plot risks appearing uninspired. 
- The Fellowship. The characters are part of a company, faction, noble house or order, and the campaign is about surviving and perhaps thriving in some domain. City adventures are common here, and the techniques for sandboxing (below) often come in handy.

You can use the adventure phases on a higher level to organize the campaign. For example, in a journey campaign, there may be one or two preparatory adventures to assemble the necessary information, like stealing a map or breaking out a guide from prison.

## Sandboxes

The opposite of having a plot is to run the campaign as a sandbox. This means that you create a map and populate it with places, organizations and individuals. Heed the advice for random tables, and also prepare a few events. Then let things play out according to the players’ choices and the dice. 

Make sure that you prepare most of the sandbox *before* starting the game. The most common fallacy from an orthodox perspective on sandboxes is probably to prepare dungeons, villages etc. but place them on the map *after* the characters have decided on a route. This means that the players didn’t actually have a choice in where the characters were going. 

Reputation, modus rolls and macros (pp. 112 ff.), as well as the rules for randomized events and reactions (p. 105), are powerful tools in a sandbox context, because they lessen the amount of planning you need to do and give the world a life of its own.

## Linking, Arch Enemies & Competition

Adventures can be linked by more than plot. Let the characters return to places they have been before. Let them meet Referee characters more than once during different parts of their careers. If a vanquished enemy flees, let her return in an adventure later on. Let the characters own houses and businesses. 

The sense of a meaningful story in a role-playing game is very much reinforced by game world consistency (not to be mistaken for status quo). But you don’t have to plan everything ahead. Save your notes as you play, and re-use them later on. A player choice can have consequences twenty sessions later, *but you don’t have to know which consequences in advance*. 

Arch enemies are excellent for linking. They are recurring, higher level bosses with two or more stages (p. 122). They should be fairly obvious, have signature items, minions, behaviors, smells and ways of speaking, and tend to *thwart and humiliate* the characters rather than killing them. 

Another linking device is a competing party of adventurers with similar interests. The relation to the party can sometimes be adversarial, but at other times cooperative. The competition can generate adventure ideas, supply replacement characters and sometimes add some love story elements to the campaign.

## Reputation

Tracking reputation for individual characters and the party as a whole can be a great help for the Referee in campaigns, in particular when it is tied to randomized events and reactions (p. 105). 

In *Whitehack*, reputation isn’t a character stat per se, but something related to the character while actually belonging to regions, groups and contexts in the campaign world. Therefore, place reputation numbers on your (mind) maps, not on the character sheets. This way, you can apply modifications to reputation rolls based on distance or connections between places or nodes. For example, if a character is a known trouble-maker in a part of the town, you could give −2 for a reputation roll ten blocks away. 

A reputation score is simply a 1–20 number, used and interpreted as in a task roll. Upon recognition, use quality to settle what is known. Only roll when it might matter or be fun. 

Reputation scores are lowered and raised at the Referee’s discretion, typically −1 per unnoticeable month and +1–6 per noticeable deed. A character who moves in a context where she is known maintains her reputation score(s) in that context. Spending a huge amount of funds is a sure way to draw attention. In particular, when a character donates or pays for training, the sums can get very high, and the reputation scores should reflect that. 

When in doubt about reputation, try to imagine the equivalent in our world. For example, what if someone rich started hiring tutors, insisted on being around “where things happen” and donated huge sums to the local library in order to get a whole section to herself? A lot of people would notice and recognize such a person.

## Threads, Rhythm, Peaks & Climax

A good campaign has several threads—side quests, parallel events, intruding problems, etc.—that are interweaved and vary play. A certain way to achieve this is to combine two plots, such as when a fellowship undertakes a journey and is tested and transformed en route. Give one thread a greater weight to not lose contour. 

Good campaigns also have rhythm, interspacing action with less intense periods and room for character development and training. This makes intense moments stand out (and if you don’t they are often lessened). Aim to make the intense moments more frequent the closer you get to a peak or to a climax.

A peak is a *major* turning point in a game story, where the characters get to make irrevocable choices, like letting a boss live or die, picking one path of no return over another, or choosing sides in a conflict. A climax on the other hand is more emotional release, usually in a campaign finale. Both require build-up, true choice, relevant change and room to come down afterwards. You may *anticipate* the outcomes of peaks and climaxes, but never enforce them.

## Modus Rolls

Finally, you can give the campaign some will of its own. A modus roll can reconfigure an adventure phase slightly as the characters enter it, making them pick a different approach. The confrontation phase is excepted, and there can only be one successful modus roll per session. 

Save vs. 10, and roll on the below table if you fail. For example, when the party enters some dungeon (an exploration phase), they may get “innocent bystanders.” You as the Referee must plausibly include it. Perhaps you add an archæologic dig or a mutant family living on the first level? Unless the session is nearing its end, you need to improvise and use the resources in the rules. 

The interpretation formula is [adventure + phase + roll], i.e. the result of a modus roll may be interpreted differently depending on the adventure and the phase. Aim for a combination of complication and opportunity that can make the characters re-evaluate their modus operandi.
