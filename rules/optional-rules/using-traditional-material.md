# Using Traditional Material

The game can run material in the original rules tradition, old or new, *without actual conversion*. Just read the material like this:

- Ignore hp entries, attack bonuses, skill values and damage dice. Instead, read the monster **HD entries** in any module and use them with *Whitehack* monster rules (p. 120). The single HD entry will give you the things you just ignored, calibrated for these rules. 
- If you are playing a module where the legacy DF values seem extreme, give all characters a module specific AV modification (+1 or +2 are common choices). In modules designed for levels 11 and up, you may also want to lower monster HD values a bit. 
- If there are attribute scores in the 3–18 range, use them directly as if they were for Whitehack. (Otherwise, you can emulate them too from the HD.) 
- Treat the names of spells and non-player character abilities as miracle wordings, triangulating their use and paying HP for them, as per the Wise rules.

This is all you need to do, and after a little while, you will notice how it all comes automatically, as a way of reading rather than converting. At that point, you can treat any traditional module as if it was written directly for *Whitehack*.

## Crawling Dungeons & Hexes

The term “turn” has a different meaning in old school modules. In dungeons, a turn is the time it takes to search a room, pick a lock, disarm a trap etc., i.e. 10 minutes (p. 53). You are generally supposed to roll for random encounters every second turn. On a d6, a 1 or 1–2 (depending on the circumstances and the party’s actions) means an encounter takes place. You also move a certain distance in the dungeon during a turn, as shown in the Crawling Rates table (p. 54). If you want to get the most out of such a module, **you need to track 10-minute periods**. You should also take special care to emphasize player skill as described in the Core Rules chapter (p. 47). 

In the wilderness, however, a turn can be a varying amount of time. For longer travels or exploration tours, many old school modules have maps with overlays of numbered hexagons. The Referee has the full map with a key telling her what the characters may encounter in each hex (or she can use random tables), while the players have an incomplete map that they fill in as they go. To move between hexes, the players tell the Referee the travel direction (N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW). The amount of hexes you can move in one go depends on the means of transportation, the terrain, the scale of the map and, finally, the length of the wilderness turn. For example, on a map where the hexes are 6 miles, and the characters move at a maximum rate of 12 miles per day’s journey (p. 53), a wilderness turn of three hours is a solid choice. Like in dungeons, random tables play a great role and are often time triggered. You need to track wilderness turns. 

If the party doesn’t have a guide, a compass or some other aid, the Referee can make a hidden, trained roll for the party’s highest intelligence or willpower, to see if the characters get lost. In such a case, the Referee chooses an adjacent hex to the one where the characters would have ended up on a success. The discrepancy between the “player route” and the actual route may increase further, or be corrected, in future wilderness turns.

## Hirelings & Retainers

Hirelings and retainers that the characters bring along for different purposes like holding torches, carrying gear or fighting by their side, play a greater role in traditional modules. In *Whitehack*, you get that kind of help through affiliation groups (p. 42), and it is the Referee who decides—based on reputation (p. 112) and sometimes a charisma roll for the character with the affiliation group—how many will come, how much compensation they require and how loyal they are in the face of death. All such hirelings and retainers are HD 1 Referee characters (but see p. 84 for more powerful ones).

## Morale Checks

In *Whitehack*, the Referee rules on fight or flight for her characters, but she may use a *morale check* to decide. Roll a d20 against the monster(s) morale value (p. 120) at the first “monster side” casualty and again when their numbers are halved. A failure indicates flight or surrender. Circumstances in combat—fog of war, proximity to a fallen comrade, getting charged by the enemy etc.—often make it plausible to have a group of enemies in one part of the battle roll together, while the rest make individual rolls.

In the case of retainers, the *player* makes a charisma roll for the employer character. This roll can be modified based on reputation (p. 112) and what the character says and does.

## Miniatures & Terrain

Though it is by no means required in order to play, *Whitehack* has full rules for the use of miniatures and grids (1 inch = 5 feet). For many players, especially if they are into miniatures, game aids contribute a lot to the fun and variation of the game.

There is a traditional toolkit that you may want to consider. Use a battle mat as your grid, and domino tiles to quickly outline the terrain. Let each character and major villain or monster have her own miniature, but use coins for lesser minions. This toolkit has the advantage of being very fast in play and comparatively cheap. It is easy on the Referee and to a degree three-dimensional. Don’t make it the Referee’s job to handle the dominoes. If she says: “Give me a room of about 20 × 20 feet with an altar in it,” the players can create it.

## Traditional Magick

Magick in the original rules tradition is hard coded, while in *Whitehack*, the boundaries for a certain miracle wording are never completely set. Though the negotiations and rulings on HP costs, and the increasing number of precedents within the gaming group, will soon develop into something steady enough, some modules may become too easy or too hard when you play them with characters who know other magick than the modules were written for.

If you intend to play a lot of such material, consider using the level entry in the traditional magick effects table (p. 38) as a guidance for HP costs, and add saves and HD restrictions to magick that might short-cut an adventure and turn challenges into walks in the park. Typical culprits are effects that let you pass through walls, read thoughts, scry and easily debilitate enemies. As a consequence, low level characters won’t have the hp to interpret such miracle wordings in a high level manner—at least not to the point where it will ruin the module. This way, you can have both the freedom of *Whitehack* and some of the rigidity of traditional magick.

## True Miracles

Another method of dealing with traditional magick is to emphasize the use of scrolls over miracle wordings:

- Give each wise character an extra slot, from the start. 
- Use scrolls as written and allow for the Wise to cram an extra use per day out of each slotted scroll, at the cost of d6−2 corruption levels (p. 76). 
- All actual miracle wordings work like normal, but are “true:” players only get to invent the wise character’s first wording. All other wordings are written by the Referee as part of her adventure material. Each true miracle wording **can only be known by a single wise individual** at any one time. It can be stolen in a wise mental struggle using either willpower or intelligence, played out as an auction (which you can embellish as a magick duel).

Consider making the Clever (p. 82) a more common class. You might also want to let the Strong loot slotted, non-combat oriented scroll effects from Wise victims, as if they were supernatural abilities rather than miracles (see p. 34).
